Highest priority

Appears in almost every exam

Master these seven first — together they account for the bulk of the points on every past paper.

1Continuous random variables: pdf, cdf, moments

  • Verify that f(x) is a proper density (integral equals 1, find the normalizing constant c)
  • Compute E(X), Var(X), E(g(X)) for transformations g
  • Derive the cdf F(x) from the pdf and use it for P(X>a), P(a<X<b)

Material: LN chap2_part1 chap2_part2 · Ex exercise_set0 exercise_set1

2Simulation: inverse transform method

  • Derive F−1(u) analytically
  • Write the algorithm in words, not just code: simulate U ∼ Unif(0,1), then X = F−1(U)
  • Typical targets: custom pdf, Weibull, Pareto, Exponential
Recurring trap — given several candidate R functions, identify which one correctly implements F−1(U). Watch for sign errors, a wrong runif interval, or a mis-transcribed formula. Seen in h22, h23, h24, v23.

Material: LN chap3_part1 · Ex exercise_set1

3Accept–Reject (AR) method

  • Choose a proposal g(x) and find the envelope constant c = max f(x)/g(x)
  • Algorithm: propose X ∼ g, accept with probability f(X)c·g(X)
  • Compare proposals — a smaller c means fewer rejections
  • Spot a buggy R implementation: constant-denominator vs. proposal-density mix-ups
Classic bug — h22 problem 1e mixes up dividing by a constant vs. dividing by the proposal density evaluated at the draw. Read the denominator carefully.

Material: LN chap3_part2 · Ex exercise_set1 · R acceptance-rejection_examples.R AR-example.R

4Monte Carlo methods for integrals

  • Crude MC — write I = E[g(X)] under a known density; estimate by averaging g(Xi)
  • Generalized MC — non-standard domains / improper integrals: pick the right auxiliary distribution (Exponential, Gamma, Uniform, Normal, Laplace…)
  • Importance sampling — works well when the proposal's tails dominate the integrand's; fails otherwise
  • Antithetic variables — pair U with 1−U (or −X with X); helps for monotone integrands, not otherwise
  • Standard error of an MC estimator: sd(g(X))√n

Material: LN chap4_part1 chap4_part2 · Ex exercise_set2 · R MC_integration_examples.R Variance_reduction.R importance_sampling.R

5Bootstrap

  • Non-parametric bootstrap: resample with replacement from the observed data
  • Estimate bias, standard error (SE), and MSE of an estimator; bias-corrected estimate
  • Three interval types — know all the formulas and how they differ:
    • Normal: θ̂ ± 1.96·SEboot
    • Percentile: the 2.5% / 97.5% quantiles of the bootstrap distribution directly
    • Basic: 2θ̂ − q0.975 to 2θ̂ − q0.025

Material: LN chap6_part1 chap6_part2 · Ex exercise_set3 · R bootstrap_examples.R

6MCMC — Metropolis–Hastings

  • Recognize the algorithm from R code:
    • Independence sampler — proposal ignores the current state; look for importance-weight-style wt.old / wt.star
    • Random walk M‑H — proposal = current state + symmetric noise, e.g. rnorm(mean = x[t-1])
  • Acceptance probability: α = mintarget(x*)target(xt−1)
  • Log-scale proposal log(θ*) = log(θt−1) + ε — remember the Jacobian term in α
  • Independent Gamma proposal centered on the MLE
  • Diagnostics: trace plot, acceptance rate (too low/high is bad), effective sample size (ESS), burn-in, thinning

Material: LN chap11_part1 chap11_part2 chap11_part3 · R mcmc_1.R mcmc_2.R mcmc_3.R

7Bayesian statistics

  • Build the likelihood L(θ) from iid data
  • Derive the posterior kernel — on the log scale — log π(θ|x) ∝ log L(θ) + log p(θ)
  • Classic conjugate pairs: Gamma–Exponential, Gamma–Poisson
  • Non-conjugate cases need MCMC (RWMH or Gibbs) to sample the posterior
  • Gibbs sampler — derive full conditionals (e.g. Normal–Normal–LogNormal model for μ and σ²) and describe the algorithm

Material: LN bayes.pdf chap12.pdf · Ex exercise_set5 exercise_set6

8Poisson processes

  • Homogeneous process: distribution of N(t); inter-arrival times ∼ Exponential(λ); time of the k‑th event ∼ Gamma(k, λ)
  • Non-homogeneous process: time-varying intensity λ(t) — know its interpretation
  • Simulate via thinning: accept–reject on arrival times of a homogeneous process with rate λmax
  • Conditional probabilities, e.g. P(N(0.1)=0 | N(1)=8)

Material: LN chap8.pdf · Ex exercise_set4 · R rain.R

9Maximum likelihood estimation (MLE)

  • Build L(θ) and the log-likelihood from an iid sample
  • Derive the MLE analytically (set the derivative to 0)
  • Check unbiasedness, compute the estimator's variance
  • Find the sample size n needed for a given margin of error at a given confidence level

Material: LN chap6_part1 · Ex exercise_set3

10Kernel Density Estimation vs. histogram

  • Pros/cons of histograms vs. KDE, and what they share
  • Bandwidth effect: too small → noisy; too large → oversmoothed
  • Automatic bin-width rules (e.g. Scott's rule) — know when they're appropriate

Material: LN chap6_part2 · R KDE.R

11Reliability systems & lifetime distributions

  • Series / parallel systems: S = min(X1,…,Xn) or max(X1,…,Xn)
  • Exponential is a special case of Weibull — use that to simplify simulation
  • Simulate a system lifetime by combining component distributions

Material: R robot_system.R production_profile.R

12Central Limit Theorem & confidence intervals

  • Approximate distribution of X for large n
  • Construct a 95% CI for a mean or function of the mean
Classic trap — many candidate CI expressions in R; pick the one where the point estimate and its standard error actually match. Seen in h22.

Material: cumulative topic — no single dedicated file

13Simulating multivariate / correlated variables

  • Multivariate normal via Cholesky decomposition
  • Compare Cholesky (direct/iid) vs. Gibbs vs. random-walk M‑H for the same target: efficiency, autocorrelation, iid vs. chain

Material: R bivariate_normal.R multivariate_normal_example.R

14Markov chains & stochastic processes

  • Simple random walk — is it Markov? Why?
  • State space and stationary distribution: existence and reasoning
  • Mean and variance of a random walk over time

15Simulation via composition / transformation

  • Lognormal from Normal (exponential transform)

How to use this roadmap

Five habits that map directly onto how these exams are actually built.

  1. For every topic in the highest-priority tier, redo at least two exercises from old exams\ without looking at the solution, then check against the matching ..._solution.pdf.

  2. Write algorithms out in pseudocode by hand, not just in your head — many questions explicitly ask for "words and formulas, not code."

  3. Drill "which of these R functions is correct" questions — they recur constantly. Check integration limits, the parameters passed to runif / rnorm / rgamma, and whether the code actually matches the formula.

  4. For MCMC, practice instant recognition: independence sampler vs. random-walk M‑H vs. Gibbs — and judging chain quality from a trace plot plus acceptance rate and ESS.

  5. Review the standard distribution relationships — Exponential ↔ Weibull ↔ Gamma, Normal ↔ Lognormal ↔ χ² — they get exploited constantly to simplify simulation.
