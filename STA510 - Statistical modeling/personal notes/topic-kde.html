13Kernel Density Estimation vs. histogram

Both are just bias-variance tradeoffs with a different knob — bin-width h for the histogram, bandwidth h for KDE — and both can be fooled the same way.

13.1Histogram

f̂(x) = νk/(nh) for tk≤x<tk+1, bins of width h, νk = count in bin k

Always a proper density (integrates to 1). But biased for any non-constant f — each bin reports the local average of f, not its value at a point — with:

bias ∝ h  (grows with h)  ·  Var(f̂(x)) ≈ f(x)/(nh)  (shrinks with h)

13.2Kernel density estimation (KDE)

f̂K(x) = 1nΣi=1n 1hK(x−Xih)

Every data point contributes a small "bump" of shape K (usually the standard normal density) centered on itself, scaled by bandwidth h; the estimate is the average of all n bumps. Also always a proper density. Same bias-variance shape as the histogram, just with a different constant:

bias ∝ h²f″(x)/2  ·  Var(f̂K(x)) ∝ 1/(nh)

13.3Bias-variance tradeoff, and how h is chosen

Small h: low bias, high variance (jagged, noisy) — too many bins / too little smoothing.
Large h: low variance, high bias (flat, oversmoothed) — too few bins / too much smoothing.

Minimizing (mean-integrated squared error, MISE=∫MSE(f̂(x))dx) trades the two off. Since the optimum depends on the unknown f itself, practical rules plug in a normal approximation:

Scott's rule (histogram): ĥ ≈ 3.49σ̂n−1/3
Sturges' rule (histogram, R's default): ĥ = (max−min)/(1+log₂n)
KDE rule of thumb: ĥ ≈ 1.06σ̂n−1/5 (for a Gaussian kernel)

13.4When both fail

All three rules above assume the data is close to normal. Two situations break that assumption:

  • Bounded/skewed data (e.g. Exponential, or any distribution with a hard boundary): KDE spreads probability mass past the boundary (e.g. below 0 for an Exponential), because a symmetric kernel doesn't know about the constraint. The histogram is more robust to this, though still biased near the edge.
  • Multimodal/mixture data (e.g. two well-separated normal components): a bandwidth tuned for overall smoothness tends to either blur the two modes together (too large) or make each mode noisy (too small) — there's no single h that's right everywhere.
h24 asks directly whether Scott's rule is advisable for n=10000 simulated Pareto(4,2) draws. Answer: no — Pareto is heavily right-skewed with a hard lower boundary at a=4, nothing like the normal shape Scott's rule is calibrated for; it will not adapt to the sharp cutoff and will oversmooth the peak near the boundary.

13.5Comparing KDEs at different bandwidths (h23-style)

Given several KDE curves overlaid on the same histogram (e.g. of simulated reliability-system lifetimes from the Reliability page) with different bandwidths: the widest/smoothest curve has the largest h (low variance, more bias — may hide real structure like a mode or a sharp edge); the most wiggly curve has the smallest h (low bias, high variance — likely fitting noise). Pick the one that traces the histogram's overall shape without following every random bump.

13.6Common exam pitfalls

  • State the shared idea first (both are biased, both trade bias for variance via a smoothing parameter) before listing differences — questions often ask for "what they have in common" explicitly.
  • Justify a bandwidth choice by its effect on bias vs. variance, not just "it looks smoother/rougher".
  • Normal-reference rules (Scott, KDE rule of thumb) are calibrated for normal-like data — flag them as questionable whenever the underlying distribution is visibly skewed, bounded, or multimodal.
