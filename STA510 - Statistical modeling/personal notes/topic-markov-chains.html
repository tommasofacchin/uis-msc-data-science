9.1The Markov property

P(Xn+1∈A ∣ Xn=c, Xn−1=a₁,…) = P(Xn+1∈A ∣ Xn=c)

Tomorrow's distribution depends on the past only through today. A chain is fully described by its transition distribution Q(x): Xn+1∣Xn ∼ Q(Xn).

Markov: Xn+1=aXn+εn+1, εn+1 iid — depends only on Xn
Not Markov: Xn+1=aXn+bXn−1+εn+1 — depends on Xn−1 too

Simulating any Markov chain, once you know Q, is three lines: fix X₁, then repeatedly draw Xn+1∼Q(Xn).

9.2Stationarity

A chain is (weakly) stationary if the distribution of Xn∣X₁=a converges to a fixed distribution π as n→∞, for every starting value a. π is the stationary distribution.

9.3Worked example — random walk (not stationary)

X₁=a, Xn+1=Xn+ξn+1, ξ iid with P(ξ=+1)=p, P(ξ=−1)=1−p (v26 asks exactly this, with a symmetric-vs-asymmetric follow-up). Since Xn=a+Σi=2nξi is a sum of n−1 iid terms:

E(ξ)=2p−1,  Var(ξ)=1−(2p−1)²=4p(1−p)

E(Xn) = a + (n−1)(2p−1),  Var(Xn) = (n−1)·4p(1−p)

Two cases, both non-stationary: if p=0.5 (symmetric), the mean stays at a but Var(Xn)=n−1 → ∞. If p≠0.5 (asymmetric/drift), the mean itself diverges to ±∞ as well. Either way there is no fixed limiting distribution — a random walk is the standard example of a Markov chain that is not stationary.

Is it Markov? Yes — Xn+1 depends on the past only through Xn (the increment ξn+1 is independent of history). State space: all integers reachable from a with the same parity pattern as n.

9.4Worked example — Gaussian AR(1) (stationary)

Xn+1 = γ + φXn + εn+1, ε iid N(0,σ²). If |φ|<1:

Xn∣X₁=a → N(γ1−φ, σ²1−φ²) as n→∞

Contrast with §9.3: the extra "pull back toward the mean" term φXn (with |φ|<1) keeps the variance from exploding — this is exactly why AR(1) is the standard building block for stationary Markov chains, and exactly the proposal mechanism behind random-walk Metropolis-Hastings on the MCMC page.

9.5Why stationarity matters (preview of MCMC)

1TΣn=1T g(Xn) → ∫g(x)π(x)dx  (a law of large numbers for stationary chains)
But Var(T⁻¹Σg(Xn)) ≠ T⁻¹Var(g(Xn)) — the Xn's are correlated, so you can't just divide by T the way you would for iid samples. This is precisely why MCMC needs the Effective Sample Size (ESS) instead of the raw chain length when building a CI for E(θ).

9.6Common exam pitfalls

  • "Is it Markov?" always has a one-line justification: identify exactly what Xn+1 depends on, and check whether that's only Xn.
  • Stationarity is about the limiting distribution — a chain can be Markov without ever settling down (random walk).
  • When asked for E(Xn)/Var(Xn) analytically, write Xn as a sum of the initial value plus iid increments first — the linear-combination rules then apply directly.
